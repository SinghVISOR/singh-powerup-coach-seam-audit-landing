The AI-Operations Playbook

Worried AI will do something dumb with your business? Good. Let's fix that.

You keep hearing you're behind on AI. Maybe you've tried a tool or two. But here's the worry that actually keeps you up: what if it emails a customer the wrong thing, or sends a quote that's off, while you're not looking? It's your name on the door, not the software's. So this playbook fixes that first. Nothing that matters goes out without your okay. You point AI at the parts of the business that eat your time, and you approve anything that counts before it happens. Here are the moves to start this week.

The HUMAN Framework

Five simple habits that keep you in charge when you hand work to AI. Not prompt tricks. Just five decisions, spelled H-U-M-A-N, that become second nature once they stick. Below, each one in plain terms, with what it looks like in a real business.

H

Halt

Put a deliberate gap between the impulse and the tool

AI is always ready to go, and that rush is catching. Before you know it you're making real decisions at its speed, and you end up with nine tools and no clear idea what any of them changed. Halt just means this: pause for a beat before you reach for it.

What it looks like: in your inbox, you stop before letting AI auto-reply to overnight emails, because one is from your biggest client and deserves you, not a template. In sales, you pause before firing a follow-up to a lead you already know is a bad fit. A two-second gap, so the person catches up to the machine.

One owner, Priya, ran an experiment that was almost insultingly simple. For thirty days, before she opened a single AI tool, she took sixty seconds — the Operator's Check — and answered three questions: What state am I in right now? What am I actually trying to produce? And if the wisest version of me were sitting here, what would she want me to reach for? Sixty seconds. No tool until the sentence was done. By week two she had turned off four of her nine tools — not because they were bad, but because she finally noticed they were answering questions she'd never actually asked. By week four she was doing less, and for the first time in a year she could tell you exactly what it changed.
The Operator's Check — 60 seconds before any AI tool
  1. What state am I in right now?
  2. What am I actually trying to produce?
  3. If the wisest version of me were sitting here, what would they want me to reach for?
The practice for the H Before you open your next AI tool — the very next one, today — take sixty seconds and write one line you will never show anyone: "Right now I am ______, and what I actually want from this is ______." No line, no tool.
In SMB.AI: the conscious pause before you run a skill. Halt is yours to keep — the software can't do it for you, and that's the point.
U

Understand the Real Question

A search-box question gets a search-box answer

Most people use AI like a search box: type a question, grab the first answer, move on. But a lazy question gets a lazy answer. Ask it to "write me a marketing plan" and you'll get beige, generic filler you can't use.

Here's the difference. The first leader stops at four seconds and takes whatever comes back. The second spends four minutes first. They tell the machine who the customer actually is, what they've already tried, what they're afraid of, what they will absolutely not do. And then they hand the machine its non-negotiables: "Never recommend discounting. Never assume a budget we don't have. If you're not sure, say so instead of inventing." Same tool. What comes back is sharp enough to use.

A founder named Jamal was drowning in AI-generated "strategy" — reports, decks, analyses, all produced in minutes, none of it landing. Asked what decision he was actually trying to make, he went quiet, then said: "Honestly? I'm trying to decide whether I still trust my own judgment, or whether I'm hoping the machine will hand me a spine." That wasn't a strategy question, and no prompt on earth was going to answer it. But the second he named the real one, the right use of the tool became obvious — and so did the part he needed to keep for himself.
The practice for the U Before your next real prompt, write the honest decision underneath it in one sentence: "What I'm actually trying to decide is ______." Then, and only then, give the machine the context and the non-negotiables. The quality of your answer will never exceed the quality of the question you were brave enough to ask.
In SMB.AI: a well-framed brief. This is where your prompt frameworks live — a God Mode priming block, SMEAC for a project, BLUF for a summary. The SMB.AI Prompting Toolkit is the toolkit for this one step.
M

Mark the Line

Draw the line between machine work and human work, on purpose, every time

Every task has a line running through it. On one side is the work that should go to the machine — summarizing, drafting, aggregating, deduplicating, the tireless repetition that used to eat your Tuesdays. On the other side is the work that must stay human — the judgment, the relationship, the values call, the moment where someone needs to know a person was actually on the other end. The Conscious Operator draws that line on purpose. The Reactive Operator lets the machine erase it.

Give to the machineSummarize · draft · aggregate · dedupe · generate options · handle the repetition at scale.
Keep humanDeciding what matters · the judgment and the values call · the relationship · the final word.
A leader named David — good man, fast operator — got an upset email from a key client, a real one, a relationship on the edge. Being efficient, he had the machine write the reply. Technically perfect: empathetic-sounding, well-structured, flawless. He sent it in ninety seconds and felt productive. The client wrote back one line: "Did you even read what I said, or did a robot answer me?" The machine hadn't made a mistake. David had. The drafting could have been the machine's. The caring had to be his — and he gave it away without noticing it was his.
The practice for the M Take one task on your plate today and draw the line straight through it out loud: "This part goes to the machine. This part stays with me." If you can't find a part that stays with you — look harder, because that's usually the part that mattered.
In SMB.AI: read-only work runs instantly; anything that sends, posts, pays, or files queues for you. The product draws the line in software — but you decide where it sits.
A

Apply the Leverage

Once the line is drawn, use the machine boldly on its side

Don't hear this whole thing as "be afraid of the machine." That is not the message. Once you've drawn the line, use the machine boldly on its side of it. There's a second, quieter failure mode than tool-sprawl: the leader so cautious, so precious about doing everything themselves, that they keep using a supercomputer like a notepad. They Halt beautifully, Understand the question, Mark the line, and then timidly ask the machine to fix a typo. That's not conscious. That's just slow with extra steps.

Renata — an author and consultant, one person, no team — was doing everything by hand out of a kind of pride. Once she drew the line clearly, she handed the machine everything on its side: first drafts, research synthesis, repurposing one talk into ten pieces, the admin that used to eat her Sundays. Same forty hours. Roughly ten times the output on the machine side. And she got more human, not less — because all the hours the machine gave back, she poured into the two things only she could do: the actual thinking and the actual relationships. Leverage on one side buys presence on the other.
The practice for the A Name one thing you are still doing by hand that lives entirely on the machine's side of the line — and this week, hand it over completely. Not half. Completely. Then take the time it gives back and spend it on the one thing only you can do.
In SMB.AI: the skills are the leverage — run them fully on the machine's side. The six areas below are where to point them.
N

Never Outsource the Core

If a machine can generate it, a machine can copy it — what can't be copied is you

There is a small, non-negotiable core of your leadership that must never go to a machine, no matter how good it gets. Your judgment. Your values. The final call. And your purpose — the reason any of this exists. The machine can inform all of it. It can never own any of it.

A truth from the frontier: the names, frameworks, and clever solutions a machine generates for you, broadly speaking, you can't own, and neither can anyone else. If your entire moat is something a machine can generate, you don't have a moat. You have a puddle the next person can generate too. What's actually yours is the same short list every time: your judgment, your taste, the trust you've earned, the relationships only you are inside of, the story only you have lived, and your purpose.

A leader named Marcus let a model make a call a model should never make — a decision about a person, dressed up as a decision about data. The model was confident, articulate, and wrong in the one way a machine is always wrong: it had no skin, no stake, and no soul in the outcome. The machine didn't seize that decision. Marcus offered it — because the model was so fluent and so sure of itself that letting it decide felt like efficiency instead of what it was: an abdication. That is how the core gets outsourced. Not in one dramatic moment, but in a hundred small Tuesdays where the machine's confidence is a little more convenient than your own responsibility.
The practice for the N Write down your short list — the handful of things in your leadership you will never outsource to a machine, no matter how convenient it becomes. Judgment. Values. The final call. Your purpose. Keep the list somewhere you'll see it. The day the machine offers to take one off your hands won't feel like a threat — it will feel like a favor. That's exactly why you write the list today.
In SMB.AI: the approval gate. Every consequential action shows you the exact content and waits for your click. The final call is always, structurally, yours.
Don't boil the ocean

Getting started — your first 30 days

You don't adopt six areas at once. You crawl, then walk, then run — one habit at a time, each built on the HUMAN framework so it sticks.

Days 1–10 · Crawl

One area, read-only

  • Pick the area that hurts most (usually Money or Inbox).
  • Run the Operator's Check before every session.
  • Use AI only for read-only work: summaries, lookups, drafts you don't send.
  • Write your "never outsource" short list.
Days 11–20 · Walk

Add the approval loop

  • Turn on one write/send skill (invoice chase, a reply draft).
  • Every send goes through the approval queue — you read the exact content.
  • Practice Mark the Line on one task a day, out loud.
  • Note the first metric that's moving.
Days 21–30 · Run

Apply the leverage

  • Hand over one thing you still do by hand — completely.
  • Add a second area; keep the ones that earned their place.
  • Set the weekly & monthly rhythm (below).
  • Check your scoreboard: what actually changed?

Area 1

Customers & Sales

HubSpotGmailWeb / LinkedIn

Turn leads into booked, prepared conversations — and keep the pipeline moving without living in your CRM.

The HUMAN loop
Halt: is this a lead worth my energy today? Understand: what am I trying to decide — pursue, nurture, or pass? Apply: let AI triage new leads, enrich them, and draft the outreach. Never outsource: the actual relationship and the promise you make.
Cadence
Daily 10-min lead triage; weekly pipeline review.
Metric that proves it
Leads contacted within 24h — and how many turned into a booked call.
Give to the machineTriage the inbox for new leads, pull the full CRM picture, enrich with company + role + recent news, draft a first-touch email.
Mark the line — keep humanDeciding who's worth your time, the actual call, the promise you make, and anything that sounds like you.
Triage & enrichPull today's new leads from HubSpot. For each, give me a BLUF: who they are, company size, why they reached out, and one thing to open the conversation with. Flag anyone I should call today.
Draft — with the line drawnDraft a first-touch email to [lead]. Warm, no jargon, under 90 words. Do NOT send — queue it for my approval so I can make it sound like me.
Area 2

Money & Finance

QuickBooksGmailSheets

Get paid faster and keep the books clean — without the spreadsheet marathon.

The HUMAN loop
Understand: am I deciding "who to chase" or "can I make payroll"? Mark the line: AI drafts the chase and the report; you decide tone with a valued client and the final numbers. Never outsource: the call on a payment-plan or a write-off.
Cadence
Weekly invoice chase; monthly close & cash-flow snapshot.
Metric that proves it
Days-to-payment (average) and overdue balance trend.
Give to the machineGenerate invoices from project data, spot overdue accounts, draft the chase sequence, categorize expenses, build the month-end summary.
Mark the line — keep humanHow hard to push a good client, whether to offer a plan, and signing off on the real numbers before anything goes out.
Chase — queued for approvalList every invoice over 14 days overdue with the client, amount, and days late. Draft a firm-but-friendly chase for each. Queue them — I'll approve the ones that should go and soften the two I care about.
Cash-flow snapshotGive me a BLUF cash-flow snapshot: money in vs out this month, what's outstanding, and the one number I should worry about. Flag assumptions.
Area 3

Comms & Inbox

GmailCalendarDrive

Stay on top of the inbox and calendar — and walk into every meeting already briefed.

The HUMAN loop
Halt before the inbox runs your morning. Apply leverage to triage and draft. Never outsource the message that carries a relationship — that's David's lesson: the drafting is the machine's, the caring is yours.
Cadence
Twice-daily triage; pre-meeting brief before every external call.
Metric that proves it
Time to inbox-clear and number of "sorry for the slow reply" moments (aim: zero).
Give to the machineSort the inbox by what needs you, draft routine replies, summarize long threads, prep a one-page brief before each meeting.
Mark the line — keep humanAny reply where the relationship is the point, the hard conversation, and the decision the meeting exists to make.
Morning triageSort my inbox into: needs me today, can wait, and AI can draft. For the last group, draft replies and queue them. Don't send anything.
Pre-meeting briefBefore my 2pm with [client]: one page — history, last contact, open items, and the one decision we need to reach. BLUF at the top.

The HUMAN loop

The HUMAN loop
Apply leverage to the gathering; Never outsource the interpretation. The machine tells you what changed. You decide what it means and what to do — that's Marcus's lesson.
Cadence
Automated weekly competitor scan; monthly report on the 1st.
Metric that proves it
Decisions made from the data — reports you actually acted on, not just received.
Give to the machinePull the numbers, watch competitors, assemble the monthly report, flag anomalies and trends automatically.
Mark the line — keep humanWhat the numbers mean for your business, the strategic call, and any decision about a person dressed up as a decision about data.
Monthly reportBuild this month's report: revenue, top movers, one worry, one opportunity. BLUF first, confidence rating on each claim, and flag anything that needs my judgment.

Area 6

Admin & Compliance

DriveDocuSignShopify

Handle the contracts, documents, and paperwork that protect the business.

The HUMAN loop
Understand what you're really deciding (sign / negotiate / walk). Apply leverage to the reading. Never outsource the signature and the risk you're willing to carry.
Cadence
On-demand contract review; weekly inventory check.
Metric that proves it
Nothing slips — renewals caught, stockouts avoided, contracts read before signing.
Give to the machineRead contracts and flag non-standard clauses, auto-renewal traps, and payment terms; watch stock levels and draft reorder notes.
Mark the line — keep humanThe decision to sign, what risk you'll accept, and the relationship with the supplier or counterparty.
Contract reviewReview this contract in plain English: flag non-standard clauses, liability, auto-renewal, and payment terms. Rate each risk. This is analysis, not legal advice — tell me what to ask a solicitor.
InventoryWhich items will run out in the next 30 days given current orders? Draft reorder emails to suppliers — queue them, don't send.

Contract review is AI analysis, not legal advice. Always consult a qualified solicitor before signing.

The floor under everything

Trust, safety & guardrails

Mark the Line and Never Outsource the Core aren't just philosophy here — they're built into how SMB.AI works.

A person approves every consequential action. No email, invoice, post, or record goes out without your explicit click.
You see the exact content that will be sent — never a summary of it — before you approve.
Read-only runs instantly; write/send always queues. The line between machine and human work is enforced in software.
Your keys and data stay protected. Credentials are never exposed to the browser; tokens are encrypted at rest.
Legal and financial outputs carry a disclaimer. AI analysis is a starting point, not professional advice.
The final call is structurally yours. The core — judgment, values, purpose — can't be outsourced, by design.
